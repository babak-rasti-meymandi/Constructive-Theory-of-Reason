# Initial Consolidated Framework: Five Stages of Cognition and Mastery in Living Organisms

**Persian source title:** چارچوب پنج‌مرحله‌ای شناخت و تسلط موجود زنده  
**Document type:** Historical consolidated record of the early discussion  
**Historical position:** First consolidated document prepared during the dialogue  
**Scope stated at the time:** Biological organisms, with a focus on human beings  
**Status:** Superseded as a current summary by later formulations; retained as a historical record

## Editorial note

This document is an edited historical reconstruction of the first consolidated framework document. It preserves the structure, conceptual positions, examples, and open questions of that early document, while omitting personal and identifying anecdotes. It is not a verbatim copy of the original conversational artifact.

This record is intentionally kept separate from the current framework manuscript and from later development-history documents. Its purpose is to show what had been brought together at the first consolidation point, including ideas that were subsequently refined, qualified, or left open.

The five terms below were already being used as technical terms within a proposed conceptual model. Their meanings should not be assumed to match ordinary usage or established scientific terminology.

---

## 1. Purpose and initial premise

The discussion examined whether what ordinary language describes through words such as reason, understanding, knowing, learning, and skill might involve multiple distinguishable stages and supporting processes rather than one undifferentiated capability.

The initial framework proposed five principal stages:

1. Awareness — آگاهی
2. Cognition — شناخت
3. Knowledge — دانایی
4. Learning — یاد
5. *شهرآگاهی* — provisional English rendering: Civic/Practical Wisdom

The immediate aim was to describe the cognitive condition and capabilities of a living organism relative to different subjects. The framework was not being presented as established science, and its extension to AI or non-biological systems was explicitly deferred.

## 2. The five proposed stages in the first consolidated account

### 2.1 Awareness — آگاهی

Awareness was initially described as noticing the presence, occurrence, or existence of a phenomenon, change, or information that had not previously been within the organism's awareness.

The expression “it was not there before, and now it is” referred to the organism's awareness, not to the objective creation of the phenomenon. Radio waves and radioactivity were used to illustrate that a phenomenon may exist long before people discover or become aware of it.

Everyday examples included noticing a newly opened shop, seeing a job advertisement, hearing an unfamiliar sound, or recognizing the existence of an activity that may be relevant to one's needs.

#### A proposed role for memory

The early discussion proposed that an organism may receive information, compare it with memory, detect that it is new relative to what is available there, and thereby become aware of it.

The proposed sequence was:

1. Receiving information.
2. Comparing it with memory.
3. Detecting that it is not present in the organism's currently recognized or available information.
4. Becoming aware of the information or phenomenon.

This was a conceptual explanatory proposal, not a complete or experimentally established account of biological awareness.

The document also distinguished registering a physical signal from awareness in the proposed biological sense. A measuring instrument's registration of radiation, by itself, was not treated as equivalent to the biological process under discussion.

**Open question at this stage:** Does novelty require that information be entirely absent from memory, or can information count as new when it exists in memory but is not currently accessible?

### 2.2 Cognition — شناخت

Cognition was described as the examination of a subject after becoming aware of it, including observation, investigation, and the acquisition of initial descriptive information.

The early formulation characterized cognition as becoming acquainted with a subject and learning about its features, without necessarily having enough experience to make reliable predictions or judgments about it.

An illustrative sequence involved hearing an unfamiliar sound in a forest, searching for its source, observing a person, and then noticing that person's appearance, clothing, and behavior. Detecting the sound was awareness; examining its source and acquiring information about it was cognition.

A computer example was also used: after first noticing the existence of a computer, a person explores its interface and commands, tests inputs, and begins to acquire information about the device and its operating environment. This is an illustration of initial cognition, not yet comprehensive competence.

### 2.3 Knowledge — دانایی

Knowledge was associated with sustained observation, accumulated experience, and the use of information to understand behavior, anticipate likely situations, evaluate outcomes, and make judgments.

The early formulation emphasized prediction. A person who has observed another person's behavior, reactions, habits, and decision patterns over time may use that experience to anticipate how the person is likely to respond in a given situation.

Such predictions are not necessarily certain or infallible. New information may require previous understanding to be revised.

In the computer example, knowledge develops beyond knowing that a computer exists or recognizing its features. It includes usable competence: understanding how a system behaves in different circumstances, using it, programming, diagnosing problems, and deciding what action is appropriate.

The initial distinction was:

- Cognition: becoming acquainted with a subject and its features.
- Knowledge: using information and experience to anticipate, evaluate, and act in relation to the subject.

### 2.4 Learning — یاد

In the initial consolidated account, *یاد* was defined as going beyond retaining separate items of information or experience. It involved classifying information, connecting it meaningfully, discovering relationships, and developing new capabilities.

The computer example illustrated a person who no longer relies only on memorized commands or isolated solutions, but can compare operating systems, recognize patterns, design solutions for new problems, create tools that did not previously exist, and sometimes anticipate needs before they are explicitly articulated.

The early account also raised the possibility that learning may occur without explicit awareness of the learning process.

Examples discussed included procedural learning or memory, priming, classical conditioning, latent learning, and statistical learning. These were presented as relevant phenomena for considering learning without explicit awareness, not as proof of the five-stage framework and not as a one-to-one mapping between established psychological terms, brain structures, and the framework's technical stages.

A key unresolved issue was whether any acquisition or retention of capability is sufficient for the technical stage *یاد*, or whether this stage necessarily requires meaningful relationship discovery, generalization, and the formation of new understanding.

### 2.5 *شهرآگاهی*

The fifth stage was initially explained through the analogy of knowing a city as a living and changing environment. A person with deep familiarity with a city does not merely memorize street names and locations; the person uses experience and knowledge as routes, conditions, and circumstances change.

The early account associated *شهرآگاهی* with:

- applying knowledge and experience in real situations;
- recognizing changing conditions;
- adapting to the environment;
- choosing an appropriate response;
- using more than one possible route to reach a goal;
- combining and revising knowledge and experience;
- maintaining effective performance as circumstances change.

The stage was therefore intended to describe more than the repetition of one fixed procedure.

The later explicit requirement that a person must be able to explain what they know appropriately to others was not yet stated in this first consolidated account. That requirement was formalized in subsequent discussion and is documented in later historical records.

## 3. Subject-specific status and transfer across subjects

The first consolidated account treated *شهرآگاهی* as subject-specific rather than as a universal status of a person.

A person may have *شهرآگاهی* in one subject, cognition in another, and only awareness in a third. The model therefore does not assign one overall stage to a person without identifying the subject under consideration.

The discussion also recognized that even within one subject, different dimensions may be at different stages. A person encountering a new programming language, for example, may recognize familiar structures in one dimension, need to investigate unfamiliar concepts in another, and transfer usable knowledge in a third.

The author selected a mixed, dimension-specific account of transfer: different dimensions of a new subject may be approached from different stages because prior capabilities can transfer unevenly.

Examples involving related natural languages illustrated how knowledge of one language may help with recognizing or inferring parts of another. Such examples were illustrative; similarity does not establish full equivalence or guarantee reliable comprehension.

The provisional principle was that prior knowledge and experience may shorten the path toward more advanced capability in a new subject when relevant structural or content relationships exist. The name “Cognitive Transferability” was considered descriptive and provisional, not a finalized technical term.

The account also cautioned that being highly competent in a whole subject does not guarantee competence in every newly encountered component of it. A new neighborhood in a familiar city, or a new component in a familiar system, may still require fresh awareness and examination.

## 4. The two-layer architectural proposal

The early discussion accepted, in principle, a distinction between two layers. The details had not yet been finalized.

### Layer 1: Five principal stages

1. Awareness
2. Cognition
3. Knowledge
4. Learning
5. *شهرآگاهی*

### Layer 2: Supporting mechanisms and capabilities

Candidate supporting processes included:

- information reception;
- attention;
- memory;
- information retrieval;
- comparison;
- inference;
- evaluation and judgment;
- choice and decision-making;
- practical skill;
- explanation and knowledge transfer;
- generalization;
- pattern recognition;
- adaptation to changing conditions.

The first consolidated account did not establish that all supporting capabilities belong in one undivided layer, nor that any one of them is exclusive to a particular principal stage.

## 5. Early principles recorded

The first consolidated document recorded the following working principles:

1. The five terms are technical terms within the proposed model and must be distinguished from their ordinary meanings.
2. Awareness does not create a phenomenon; it concerns the organism becoming aware of something that may already exist.
3. Memory has a proposed role in detecting novelty.
4. Cognition involves examining and acquiring information about a subject after awareness.
5. Knowledge involves using information and experience for prediction, evaluation, and judgment.
6. Learning goes beyond retaining information and includes discovering relationships, structuring experience, generalizing, and developing capabilities.
7. Learning need not always be accompanied by explicit awareness of the learning process.
8. *شهرآگاهی* is subject-specific.
9. Different dimensions of a subject may be at different stages at the same time.
10. Knowledge and capabilities may transfer across subjects, but transfer may be partial and dimension-specific.
11. *شهرآگاهی* was treated as graded rather than strictly binary.
12. Practical competence, theoretical knowledge, and the ability to explain knowledge should not be assumed to be identical.

**Historical qualification:** Principle 12 was an early distinction, not yet the later formal rule that appropriate explanation is a necessary condition for *شهرآگاهی*. That explicit rule was adopted in subsequent discussion.

## 6. Open questions preserved at the first consolidation point

The initial consolidated document identified the following matters for further examination:

1. **Definition of awareness:** Is detecting novelty relative to memory sufficient? How should inaccessible memories be treated?
2. **Boundary between cognition and knowledge:** At what point do descriptive information and familiarity become usable experience for prediction?
3. **Boundary between knowledge and learning:** Is discovering relationships necessary for entering the stage of *یاد*? Is creating new tools a defining feature or an advanced outcome?
4. **Implicit learning:** Should every form of implicit learning be placed within *یاد*, or should retention, skill acquisition, relationship discovery, and conceptual learning be distinguished?
5. **Memory:** Is memory only a supporting mechanism, or should different forms of memory be represented separately?
6. **Explanation in *شهرآگاهی*:** What level of explanation is required, and must it include principles, relationships, limitations, and exceptions?
7. **Degrees of *شهرآگاهی*:** Could degrees depend on experience, diversity of conditions, adaptability, route selection, depth of understanding, and ability to explain?
8. **Practical skill and explanation:** Should these be treated as independent dimensions, each with a minimum threshold for *شهرآگاهی*?
9. **Cognitive transfer:** Which kinds of similarity support transfer, and can transfer occur at each of the five stages?
10. **Emotion:** Should emotional responses to starting over in a new subject be treated as an independent axis or as a supporting process?
11. **Metacognition:** Is awareness of one's own knowledge and limitations part of *شهرآگاهی*?

These questions were open in the first consolidated document. Later decisions must be read in their chronological context rather than projected backward onto this initial record.

## 7. Scope and methodological limits

The first consolidated account explicitly maintained the following limits:

- The five stages were proposed concepts under development, not an experimentally validated scientific theory.
- Established cognitive-science terms were not to be treated as direct equivalents of the framework's technical terms without analysis.
- Examples involving memory, implicit learning, and brain structures did not by themselves validate the proposed five-stage architecture.
- The immediate scope was biological organisms, especially human beings.
- Extension to artificial intelligence, non-biological tools, or engineered systems was deferred.
- A person's capabilities were not to be summarized by a single global stage; assessment was subject- and dimension-specific.
- Practical performance, theoretical understanding, and explanation ability were distinct and should not be conflated.
- No formal measurement scale for degrees of *شهرآگاهی* had yet been established.

## 8. Historical summary

At this first consolidation point, the framework had a recognizable five-stage structure and a preliminary supporting layer. It already included subject-specific assessment, uneven transfer across dimensions, implicit-learning questions, and a graded conception of *شهرآگاهی*.

However, several boundaries remained unsettled, especially the distinction between knowledge and *یاد*, the role of memory, the status of implicit learning, and the criteria for *شهرآگاهی*.

Most importantly, the explicit explanation requirement had not yet been incorporated as a necessary condition in this first consolidated document. Its later adoption represents a subsequent conceptual development, not an original feature of this document.
