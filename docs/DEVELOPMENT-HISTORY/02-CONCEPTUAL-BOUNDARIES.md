# Conceptual Decisions and Operational Boundaries: Early Development Record

**Project:** Constructive Theory of Reason  
**Source language:** Persian  
**Document type:** Structured record of distinctions and criteria raised in the early discussion  
**Status:** Historical development record; not a replacement for the primary framework manuscript

## 1. Why technical terms must be distinguished from ordinary language

The author explicitly warned that the framework's terms — **آگاهی، شناخت، دانایی، یاد، شهرآگاهی** — must not be confused with their broad, everyday meanings.

For example, in ordinary Persian, a person may say that someone “بلد است” (knows how to do something). That everyday use does not automatically establish that the person has reached the technical stage called *شهرآگاهی* in this framework.

Likewise, ordinary uses of “یاد گرفتن” or “شناختن” should not be treated as sufficient evidence that the corresponding technical stage has been attained.

**Editorial rule:** In formal documents, the technical term should be interpreted according to its framework definition, not merely according to its everyday lexical meaning.

## 2. The transfer hypothesis: more than one stage may be available in a new subject

The early discussion considered three possible accounts of transfer from a familiar subject to a new one:

- direct transfer of some cognition or knowledge because of similarities;
- progression through the stages at a faster rate;
- a mixed account in which different dimensions of the new subject may begin at different stages.

The author independently selected the mixed account.

### 2.1 Mixed, dimension-specific transfer

The working proposition is that a person entering a new subject may:
- have only awareness in one dimension;
- already have cognition in another dimension because of structural similarity;
- transfer usable knowledge in a third dimension.

The stage must therefore be assessed by subject and dimension, not assigned globally to the person.

### 2.2 Transfer is not equivalence

Prior competence may help with a new subject, but it does not prove mastery of that subject. Similarity can support recognition, inference, or faster learning without making the two subjects identical.

Examples involving related languages were used to illustrate possible transfer. The examples are illustrative and do not establish that linguistic similarity always permits reliable comprehension.

## 3. Distinguishing technical stages from performance labels

The discussion used driving and swimming to expose a possible category error: a person may be described in everyday language as “knowing how” to perform an activity while still occupying different technical stages under the proposed framework.

### 3.1 Driving as a staged example

A generalized progression was proposed:

1. **Awareness:** Recognizing driving as an activity and recognizing a personal need to learn it.
2. **Cognition:** Receiving instruction and acquiring an understanding of driving principles, controls, rules, and procedures.
3. **Knowledge:** Demonstrating usable competence and being able to anticipate and respond to familiar driving situations.
4. **Learning:** Extending competence across different vehicles, weather, road conditions, traffic patterns, times of day, and unfamiliar situations; using experience to recognize patterns and adapt.
5. **Potential *شهرآگاهی*:** A higher level of adaptable, sustained competence that also satisfies the framework's additional communication requirement described below.

The sequence is conceptual. It is not a legal classification, licensing rule, or claim that a particular period of driving automatically establishes a stage.

### 3.2 Theoretical knowledge versus embodied performance in swimming

A second example contrasted:
- a person who has studied the physics and theory of swimming but cannot swim safely;
- a person who acquired practical swimming ability through experience but cannot explain the principles or teach them to another person.

The contrast shows why theoretical familiarity, practical ability, and *شهرآگاهی* must not be conflated.

Under the author's later explicit rule, practical performance alone is not sufficient to establish *شهرآگاهی* if the person cannot appropriately explain what they know to others.

The example is conceptual and does not recommend learning to swim without qualified instruction or safe supervision.

## 4. The explanation requirement for *شهرآگاهی*

A central question was whether a person who performs exceptionally well in a changing environment, but cannot explain or teach what they know, should be considered *شهرآگاه*.

The author answered unequivocally:

> A person who cannot appropriately explain to others what they know how to do cannot claim to be *شهرآگاه*.

This is recorded as an explicit author-stated rule, not an assistant-suggested condition.

### 4.1 What the rule does and does not say

The rule establishes that:
- practical performance alone is insufficient;
- the person must be able to explain their competence appropriately to others;
- the explanation must be suitable to the subject and the audience.

The rule does not, by itself, establish that:
- every person must use the same teaching method;
- every explanation must be exhaustive or academically formal;
- the person must be able to verbalize every automatic motor action;
- one unsuccessful explanation in one circumstance proves a permanent lack of competence.

The phrase “appropriately explain” requires further operational definition. It may involve selecting relevant content, communicating it at a suitable level, and making the underlying competence sufficiently intelligible to another person. These are clarifications for future examination, not additional author-approved criteria unless separately adopted.

## 5. *شهرآگاهی* is graded, not binary

After stating the explanation requirement, the author added that *شهرآگاهی* itself has degrees, from low to high.

Therefore, the framework must not treat *شهرآگاهی* as a simple binary state.

A future operational model may need to distinguish degrees of:
- adaptability across conditions;
- breadth of situations handled;
- reliability and continuity of performance;
- ability to select among multiple routes to a goal;
- depth and appropriateness of explanation to others.

These dimensions are candidate assessment dimensions for further discussion. They should not be treated as a finalized scoring system.

## 6. Implicit learning and other cognitive phenomena: a terminology caution

The discussion mentioned examples commonly described with terms such as:
- procedural memory or procedural learning;
- priming;
- classical conditioning;
- latent learning;
- statistical learning.

These examples were raised to consider whether learning or retained capability can occur without explicit awareness.

The author then identified a potential conceptual mistake: the framework's technical stages must not be mapped directly onto everyday or established psychological labels simply because the same words are used.

**Important limitation:** The listed phenomena are not, by themselves, evidence that the framework's technical stage called *یاد* or *شهرآگاهی* has been attained. Their neural mechanisms and empirical interpretation require independent scientific sources and review. The early dialogue did not establish a one-to-one mapping between those phenomena, brain structures, and the five-stage framework.

## 7. Subject-specific assessment and cross-subject capability

The same person can occupy different stages in different subjects at the same time. Therefore, an assessment should specify:

- the subject being assessed;
- the dimension or aspect of that subject;
- the evidence of capability in that dimension;
- the stage claimed;
- whether the evidence comes from direct performance, explanation, prediction, or transfer from another subject.

This avoids treating an individual's strongest area as evidence of equivalent competence in unrelated areas.

## 8. Author-stated positions versus unresolved operational questions

### Explicit positions recorded

1. The five technical terms must not be confused with ordinary language.
2. Stages are subject-specific.
3. A person may occupy different stages in different subjects or dimensions simultaneously.
4. Capabilities may transfer across subjects in a mixed, dimension-specific way.
5. Practical performance alone does not establish *شهرآگاهی*.
6. Appropriate explanation to others is a necessary condition for claiming *شهرآگاهی*.
7. *شهرآگاهی* has degrees from lower to higher.

### Questions requiring further definition

1. What counts as “appropriate” explanation?
2. How should explanation be assessed without confusing verbal fluency with genuine competence?
3. How should *شهرآگاهی* degrees be distinguished and evidenced?
4. How can transfer be measured separately from direct competence in the new subject?
5. How should implicit or procedural capabilities be represented without collapsing the framework's technical stages into established psychological terminology?
6. Can a person have strong practical performance and weak explanatory ability, and if so, how should the framework classify the gap without ignoring either capability?

## 9. Methodological safeguards for future revisions

- Preserve the chronology of conceptual changes.
- Do not retroactively attribute later criteria to earlier formulations.
- Keep personal anecdotes and identifying details out of formal public documents unless the author explicitly requests otherwise.
- Distinguish author-stated rules from editorial interpretations and candidate assessment dimensions.
- Do not infer scientific validation from illustrative examples.
- Do not map the five stages onto AI or other non-biological systems without a separate argument and explicit scope decision.
- Keep the current framework manuscript distinct from this historical record.
