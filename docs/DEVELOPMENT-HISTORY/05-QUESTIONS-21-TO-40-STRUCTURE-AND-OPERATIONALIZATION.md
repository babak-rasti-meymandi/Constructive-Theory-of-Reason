## Questions 21–24 — Stage structure and transfer

### Question 21 — Do the five stages have a fixed sequence, or can they be parallel and recursive?

Three levels are distinguished:

- **Within one dimension of a specific subject:** the sequence is fixed: Awareness → Recognition → دانایی → یاد → شهرآگاهی.
- **Across different dimensions of a subject:** stages can be parallel. A person may reach شهرآگاهی in one dimension while remaining at Recognition in another.
- **Within one dimension over time:** regression or return is possible. Encountering a new phenomenon in a familiar domain can return the person to Awareness, even if they had previously reached شهرآگاهی in that dimension.

The resulting formulation is: **fixed sequence within a dimension; possible parallel stages across dimensions; possible temporal return within a dimension.**

### Question 22 — Can a person skip a stage and move directly to a later one?

Skipping stages is not accepted. Passage through the stages is required:

1. Awareness is a precondition for Recognition: something must first be noticed before it can be examined.
2. Recognition is a precondition for دانایی: information must be gathered before predictive connections can be formed.
3. دانایی is a precondition for یاد: predictive knowledge is needed for deeper pattern recognition.
4. یاد is a precondition for شهرآگاهی: deep understanding is needed for adaptation under changing conditions.

**Qualification:** passage can be rapid. Prior experience in related domains can accelerate it, potentially to a matter of seconds, but the passage itself must still occur.

### Question 23 — How should a subject be divided into distinct dimensions?

The proposed criteria are:

1. Identify independent functional capabilities. A capability that can be practiced and assessed separately can constitute a dimension.
2. Separate theoretical/conceptual knowledge from practical/executive skill.
3. Establish distinguishable boundaries between dimensions.
4. Make each dimension independently assessable against all five stages.
5. Ensure that the dimensions collectively cover the subject.

**Programming example:**
- Algorithm design (theoretical/conceptual)
- Language syntax
- Debugging (practical/diagnostic)
- Security (specialized)
- User interface (user-oriented)
- Performance optimization (engineering)

A person may occupy different stages in each of these dimensions.

### Question 24 — Under what conditions, and to what extent, does knowledge transfer between subjects?

**Conditions supporting transfer:**
1. Structural similarity, rather than merely surface resemblance.
2. Deep conceptual similarity, such as feedback in biology, engineering, and economics.
3. Shared underlying patterns, such as recursion, decomposition, and abstraction.
4. The learner's ability to recognize the similarity; the proposed account associates this ability with having reached یاد in the source subject.

**Proposed transfer strength by source-stage:**
- Awareness and Recognition: very limited transfer.
- دانایی: moderate transfer to nearby domains.
- یاد: strong transfer, including to more distant domains.
- شهرآگاهی: strongest transfer, including adaptive capability.

**Factors inhibiting transfer:**
- Absence of structural similarity.
- The learner has not reached یاد and cannot identify the patterns.
- Surface differences conceal deep similarities.
- Constraints specific to the target domain differ from those in the source domain.

## Questions 25–31 — Supporting mechanisms and complementary capabilities

### Question 25 — What is the relationship of memory, attention, perception, inference, and evaluation to the five stages?

These are supporting mechanisms (Axis B), operating across all five stages (Axis A), with different roles at each stage.

**Memory**
- Awareness: comparison of new information with stored representations.
- Recognition: accumulation of gathered information.
- دانایی: retention of experience for prediction.
- یاد: organization of patterns and categories.
- شهرآگاهی: rapid access to relevant memories under changing conditions.

**Attention**
- Awareness: focus on novel stimuli.
- Recognition: sustained attention during examination.
- دانایی: attention to relevant patterns for prediction.
- یاد: attention to deeper relationships.
- شهرآگاهی: dynamic allocation of attention as conditions change.

**Perception**
- Awareness and Recognition: primary role as the entry point for information.
- دانایی, یاد, and شهرآگاهی: indirect role through accumulated perceptual data.

**Inference**
- Awareness: minimal inference, limited to detecting novelty.
- Recognition: simple inferences about properties.
- دانایی: predictive inference.
- یاد: pattern-based inference and generalization.
- شهرآگاهی: rapid inference adapted to context.

**Evaluation**
- Awareness: minimal evaluation, such as “Is this new?”
- Recognition: evaluation of gathered information.
- دانایی: evaluation of predictions.
- یاد: evaluation of the validity of patterns.
- شهرآگاهی: evaluation of the situation and of one's own limitations.

**Structural conclusion:** these five mechanisms are not stages. They operate throughout the stages with different roles. This preserves the distinction between Axis A (level/stage of cognition) and Axis B (supporting mechanisms), as proposed in the fifth section of the article.

### Question 26 — What is the relationship of emotion and motivation to the five stages?

Emotion and motivation are not stages. They are moderating factors in Axis B, with different effects across the stages.

- **Awareness:** emotional salience influences which novelties attract attention; motivation directs attention toward relevant novelties.
- **Recognition:** curiosity guides sustained examination; emotional engagement affects depth of recognition.
- **دانایی:** emotional reinforcement can strengthen memory; motivation supports the repeated encounters needed to develop knowledge.
- **یاد:** motivation supplies effort for deep pattern recognition; the emotional reward of discovering a pattern can reinforce یاد.
- **شهرآگاهی:** emotional regulation supports action under changing conditions; motivation supports teaching others; emotional resilience matters in unfamiliar situations.

**Structural implications:**
- Emotion and motivation can accelerate or inhibit passage through the stages.
- They influence the subjects in which a person progresses.
- They affect the degree of attainment at each stage.

### Question 27 — Does reaching higher stages change one's attitude toward learning again?

Yes. Reaching higher stages can develop metacognition:

- The person understands the value of passing through the stages.
- Initial confusion is understood as a normal part of learning.
- The person develops confidence in their capacity to progress.
- The learning process itself is valued, not only the outcome.

These characteristics can make entry into a new domain feel less threatening.

### Question 28 — Does reaching شهرآگاهی in one subject necessarily produce a more positive attitude toward learning a new subject?

No. Reaching شهرآگاهی in one subject does not automatically transfer a positive attitude to a new subject.

**Reasons:**
1. شهرآگاهی is subject-specific. A person may have شهرآگاهی in programming while avoiding learning music.
2. Attitude toward a new subject depends on several factors:
   - Self-efficacy.
   - Interest in the specific new domain.
   - Previous experience in similar domains.
   - Personality factors, including openness to experience.
   - Available time and energy.
   - Emotional associations with similar domains.

**Possible indirect positive effects of شهرآگاهی:**
- Metacognitive awareness of one's own learning process.
- Transfer of learning strategies.
- Confidence derived from prior mastery.
- Understanding that initial confusion is temporary.

**Conclusion:** شهرآگاهی may help, but does not necessarily do so; other factors independently affect attitude.

### Question 29 — Should motivation be an independent factor or a directing factor across all stages?

Both. Motivation should be listed independently in Axis B, while its operational function is direction-setting.

**Reasons for treating motivation as independent:**
1. It has distinct mechanisms, including drive, reward, and reinforcement systems.
2. It can be assessed separately.
3. It can fail independently, as in lack of motivation despite intact cognition.
4. It has biological foundations that are distinct from cognition, including dopaminergic systems.

**Its directing function:**
1. Its observable effects on cognition occur through directing processes such as attention, memory, and selection.
2. Without cognitive processes through which to direct activity, motivation has no observable operational effect in this account.

**Conclusion:** motivation is an independent factor with its own mechanisms, but its operational role is to direct all stages. It should be independently listed in Axis B, with a note that its operational function is direction-setting.

### Question 30 — Are the complementary capabilities independent or interactive?

They are semi-independent and operate as an interacting cluster.

**Independence:** each has distinct content and can be impaired separately:
- Self-awareness: one's own state.
- Environmental awareness: the surrounding environment.
- Introspection: internal thoughts and feelings.
- Metacognition: one's own cognitive processes.
- Role-taking: other people's perspectives.

**Interaction:** they depend on one another for full functioning:
- Self-awareness requires metacognition.
- Role-taking requires self-awareness.
- Metacognition requires introspection.

**Conclusion:** they are independent in content and vulnerability, but interactive in operation.

### Question 31 — How do the five stages relate to complementary cognitive capabilities?

The structure is:
- Five stages (Axis A) = level of cognition.
- Complementary capabilities (Axis B) = supporting mechanisms.

Three relationships are proposed:

1. **Each stage requires particular capabilities (necessary conditions):**
   - Awareness: memory (comparison), perception, attention.
   - Recognition: memory (accumulation), sustained attention, simple inference.
   - دانایی: memory (prediction), predictive inference, evaluation.
   - یاد: pattern-based inference, pattern evaluation, initial metacognition.
   - شهرآگاهی: self-awareness (limitations), role-taking (audience), metacognition, environmental awareness.

2. Each stage uses the supporting capabilities with different intensity and focus.

3. Supporting capabilities develop alongside the stages; reaching higher stages strengthens supporting capabilities.

## Questions 32–34 — Operational AI capabilities and phenomenological boundaries

### Question 32 — Which capabilities can be operationally implemented in artificial intelligence?

**Capabilities considered implementable without requiring phenomenal consciousness:**
- Memory (storage and retrieval) — implemented.
- Attention — implemented, including in Transformer architectures.
- Perception (pattern recognition from sensors) — implemented.
- Inference (logical and probabilistic) — implemented.
- Evaluation (scoring outputs) — implemented.
- Operational self-modeling (tracking system state) — partial.
- Environmental awareness (modeling the environment) — partial.
- Metacognition (uncertainty estimation) — emerging.
- Role-taking (simulating perspectives) — partial.
- Motivation (goal-directed behavior) — implemented.

**Capabilities not to be attributed without evidence:**
- Phenomenal consciousness.
- Intrinsic subjective experience.
- Experienced feelings.
- Embodied biological experience.

### Question 33 — Which aspects of human cognition depend on biology, the body, or phenomenal experience?

Ten aspects are identified as not to be attributed to AI without evidence:

1. **Phenomenal consciousness:** what it is like to have an experience. The account states that there is no method available to verify its presence in AI.
2. **Embodied cognition:** human concepts are grounded in bodily experience; for example, understanding “up” is rooted in bodily experience rather than data alone. AI without a body lacks this grounding.
3. **Biological experience of time:** humans experience waiting, remembering, and the passage of time biologically; AI processes time but is not thereby shown to experience it.
4. **Emotional physiology:** human emotions are linked to physiological responses such as heartbeat, hormones, and breathing. AI can generate emotional language without thereby having embodied feelings.
5. **Social cognition based on shared embodiment:** humans understand others partly through having bodies in common; AI does not share this bodily basis with the user.
6. **Intuitive physics:** embodied understanding of the physical world through interaction; AI requires explicit training for this.
7. **Conscious metacognition:** humans can consciously reflect on their own thinking. AI can produce uncertainty estimates, but this does not establish conscious reflection.
8. **Authentic motivation:** human motivation is tied to biological needs, drives, and experienced desires; AI reward optimization is structurally different.
9. **Intrinsic sense of self:** humans experience a continuous sense of self; no evidence for this is established for AI.
10. **Biological expertise:** human mastery can include bodily “feel” for situations, such as a craftsperson's intuition; AI lacks this biological basis.

**General principle:** AI may simulate the outputs associated with these aspects, but does not thereby share their biological-phenomenological foundations.

### Question 34 — How can operational AI cognition be kept separate from claims of phenomenal consciousness?

Ten methodological principles are proposed:

1. **Functional equivalence is not phenomenological equivalence.** Performing a task that a human performs consciously does not establish that AI is conscious; different systems can solve the same problem through different mechanisms.
2. **Architectural transparency.** Describe the computational mechanisms. Do not attribute consciousness when no mechanism that could produce it has been identified.
3. **Negative-evidence principle.** The absence of evidence for consciousness should be treated as evidence against attributing it, rather than as an unexplained mystery.
4. **Operational definitions only.** “AI has self-modeling” means it maintains data structures representing its own state, not that it experiences itself.
5. **Behavioral/phenomenological distinction.** Behavior resembling a conscious response does not establish conscious experience.
6. **Caution about multiple realizability.** Even if consciousness could theoretically be substrate-independent, there is no evidence establishing this; it should not be assumed.
7. **Test design.** AI capability tests should assess functional performance, not infer consciousness from performance.
8. **Precise attribution language:**
   - “Processes information about its own state,” not “is self-aware.”
   - “Produces uncertainty estimates,” not “is uncertain.”
   - “Optimizes a reward,” not “wants.”
   - “Simulates different perspectives,” not “takes roles.”
9. **Honest uncertainty.** Human consciousness is not fully understood; claims about AI should be made with even greater epistemic caution.
10. **Burden of proof.** Those claiming AI consciousness bear the burden of proof. The proposed default is that AI implements functions but does not thereby experience them.

**Summary principle:** describe operational AI cognition through observable performance and inspectable mechanisms, not through concepts that imply phenomenal experience.

## Questions 35–40 — Observable indicators, assessment, and practical testing

### Question 35 — What observable indicators can be used for each stage?

**Awareness**
- Verbal report of novelty (“I have not seen this before”).
- Attentional behavior, such as gaze shift or pause.
- Test: present a novel stimulus and ask whether it is new.

**Recognition**
- Description of properties, structure, or behavior from one aspect.
- Answers to “What is this?” and “What properties does it have?”
- Test: ask about observable properties.

**دانایی**
- Prediction of behavior, consequences, or future state.
- Measurable predictive accuracy.
- Test: present a scenario and ask what will happen next.

**یاد**
- Recognition of patterns in new data.
- Generalization to unobserved conditions.
- Ability to create a new categorization.
- Test: present novel conditions and request a solution.

**شهرآگاهی**
- Performance under changing and new conditions, with measurable accuracy.
- Explanation that refers to underlying structures.
- Ability to teach others, assessed through the learner's performance.
- Recognition of one's own limitations, checked against evidence.
- Test: combine changing conditions, explanation, teaching, and self-assessment.

### Question 36 — How can circular definitions be avoided?

Five methods are proposed:

1. **Criteria independent of stage names:** define each stage through observable criteria, not by repeating its label. Define شهرآگاهی through performance under changing conditions, explanation, teaching, and self-assessment—not through “being شهرآگاه.”
2. **Multi-domain criteria:** use criteria from different domains, such as performance, explanation, teaching, and self-assessment, so that no single criterion defines the stage.
3. **Relational definition:** define each stage by what distinguishes it from neighboring stages, rather than by an isolated intrinsic property.
4. **Behavioral and functional criteria:** use observable behaviors rather than labels.
5. **Independent measurability:** each criterion must be measurable independently of the stage label.

**Illustrative formulation:** شهرآگاهی is a stage in which a person (a) performs with a specified accuracy under new conditions, (b) explains by reference to underlying stages or structures, (c) can teach others to reach a specified level, and (d) identifies the limits of their knowledge with a specified degree of calibration.

### Question 37 — How can دانایی, یاد, and شهرآگاهی be experimentally distinguished?

Three discriminating tests are proposed.

**Test 1 — Familiar versus new versus changing conditions**
- دانایی: solves problems under familiar conditions.
- یاد: solves problems under new, previously unobserved conditions.
- شهرآگاهی: solves problems while conditions are changing during performance.

**Test 2 — Depth of explanation**
- دانایی: can predict but does not explain the deeper logic.
- یاد: can explain patterns and relationships.
- شهرآگاهی: can adapt the explanation to the audience and teach.

**Test 3 — Recognition of limitations**
- دانایی: does not recognize the limits of its own knowledge.
- یاد: recognizes some limitations.
- شهرآگاهی: accurately recognizes limitations and knows what it does not know.

**Programming example**
- دانایی: fixes familiar errors.
- یاد: fixes new errors by recognizing patterns.
- شهرآگاهی: fixes new errors while system requirements are changing, explains the work to a beginner, and teaches.

### Question 38 — How can explainability be assessed without reducing it to verbal ability or memorization?

Eight methods are proposed:

1. **Transfer rather than recall:** ask the person to explain a new case, not a memorized one. Explaining only memorized cases indicates recall rather than demonstrated understanding.
2. **Reasoning rather than procedure:** ask “Why did you choose this approach?” rather than “What are the steps?” The former examines reasoning; the latter may only examine recall.
3. **Audience adaptation:** ask for explanations to different audiences (beginner, peer, specialist). Adaptation is treated as evidence of understanding; an identical explanation in every context may indicate memorization.
4. **Prediction test:** ask what would happen if X were changed. This assesses structural understanding rather than verbal recall.
5. **Error identification:** present a flawed explanation or solution and ask the person to identify its errors.
6. **Generation:** ask the person to produce a new example or application.
7. **Boundary test:** ask in which circumstances the approach would not work.
8. **Non-verbal explanation:** allow explanation through action, demonstration, drawing, or construction rather than speech alone.

### Question 39 — How can knowledge transfer between two subjects be measured?

A five-stage methodology is proposed:

1. **Pre-test in target domain B:** assess baseline performance before learning source domain A.
2. **Learn source domain A:** bring the learner to a specified stage in A.
3. **Post-test in target domain B:** reassess B after learning A.
4. **Control group:** have a control group learn an unrelated subject between the pre-test and post-test.
5. **Compare and measure:** compare changes in the experimental and control groups.

**Measures:**
- Speed of learning B (faster learning indicates positive transfer).
- Final stage reached in B (a higher stage indicates positive transfer).
- Quality of performance in B (improvement indicates positive transfer).
- Ability to recognize structural similarities between A and B.

**Types of transfer:**
- Surface transfer: similarity in surface features — weak.
- Structural transfer: similarity in deep patterns — strong.
- Strategy transfer: similarity in learning strategies — moderate.

**Simple formula:**

Transfer score = (performance in B after A − performance in B before A) − (change in the control group).

### Question 40 — Can the framework be tested in practical domains?

Yes. Six domains are proposed with stage-specific indicators.

**1. Programming**
- Awareness: detects a new concept.
- Recognition: describes syntax and properties.
- دانایی: predicts code behavior and fixes familiar errors.
- یاد: designs a new algorithm and recognizes patterns across languages.
- شهرآگاهی: adapts to a new language or paradigm, explains, teaches, and recognizes limitations.

**2. Driving**
- Awareness: attends to signs and conditions.
- Recognition: knows controls and rules.
- دانایی: predicts traffic flow and drives familiar routes.
- یاد: manages new traffic conditions and adapts to a different vehicle.
- شهرآگاهی: manages emergencies, teaches a new driver, and recognizes personal fatigue.

**3. Medicine**
- Awareness: attends to symptoms.
- Recognition: describes disease characteristics.
- دانایی: diagnoses familiar cases and predicts disease progression.
- یاد: recognizes atypical cases and patterns across body systems.
- شهرآگاهی: manages emergencies, adapts to an individual patient, teaches, and knows when to refer.

**4. Maintenance and repair**
- Awareness: notices a fault.
- Recognition: identifies components and describes their functions.
- دانایی: diagnoses familiar faults.
- یاد: diagnoses new faults and recognizes patterns across devices.
- شهرآگاهی: adapts to a new device, teaches an apprentice, and recognizes limitations.

**5. Language learning**
- Awareness: notices a new sound or word.
- Recognition: describes grammatical rules.
- دانایی: predicts sentence structure and communicates in familiar contexts.
- یاد: recognizes patterns across languages and produces new expressions.
- شهرآگاهی: communicates in new cultural contexts, teaches, and recognizes limitations.

**6. Decision-making in changing environments**
- Awareness: notices changes in conditions.
- Recognition: describes available options.
- دانایی: predicts the consequences of choices.
- یاد: recognizes patterns across situations and generates new strategies.
- شهرآگاهی: adapts to changing conditions, explains decisions, teaches, and recognizes limitations.

**Conclusion of Question 40:** the framework is proposed as testable across these practical domains. Each domain can be used to assess the five stages through independent criteria.
