# Development History: Early Formulation and First Conceptual Refinements

**Project:** Constructive Theory of Reason  
**Source language:** Persian  
**Document type:** Structured historical record of early dialogue  
**Coverage:** Initial formulation and the first three rounds of conceptual refinement represented in the source material currently reviewed  
**Status:** Partial historical record; not a complete transcript

## Editorial and evidentiary note

This document records the conceptual content and development sequence of the early discussion. It is not a verbatim transcript. Personal anecdotes, identifying details, informal conversational language, and incidental biographical information have been excluded. Examples have been generalized where necessary.

The record distinguishes:
- **Author position:** a position explicitly stated by the human author in the source discussion.
- **Analytical observation:** a clarification or question arising from examination of that position.
- **Open issue:** a matter that the source discussion left unresolved.

The record does not retroactively attribute later definitions to earlier statements. Nor does it treat an analytical observation as an author-approved principle unless the author explicitly adopted it.

## 1. Initial proposal: five stages of human reason

The inquiry began with a proposal that human reason (Persian: *ویر* or *عقل*) could be examined through five stages:

1. Awareness — آگاهی
2. Cognition — شناخت
3. Knowledge — دانایی
4. Learning — یاد
5. *شهرآگاهی* — a working English rendering remains unsettled

The initial formulation used the metaphor of steps or stages. It began with concrete examples and attempted to derive conceptual distinctions from them.

### 1.1 Awareness

**Initial author position:** Awareness begins when an organism notices an event, difference, or information that is new relative to its prior state of awareness.

The initial examples included:
- a light becoming visible in an otherwise dark environment;
- an organism that lacks the capacity to detect a particular stimulus;
- detection of phenomena, such as radio waves or radioactivity, that may require instruments;
- an animal being recorded by a camera without detecting that the recording occurred;
- a person becoming aware of an unfamiliar sound in an unfamiliar environment.

The key distinction was that the existence of an event does not, by itself, mean that an organism is aware of it.

### 1.2 Cognition

**Initial author position:** Cognition begins when the organism examines an object or person encountered and acquires information about its features.

The example of encountering an unfamiliar person emphasized noticing appearance, clothing, and behavior. The person may then be recognized or known in a preliminary sense without yet being understood well enough to support reliable judgments about them.

This established an early distinction between recognizing or becoming acquainted with something and being knowledgeable about it.

### 1.3 Knowledge

**Initial author position:** Knowledge involves using previously acquired information and observations to understand patterns, anticipate behavior, and make judgments.

The early account included:
- observing behavior over time;
- using previous observations to anticipate likely behavior;
- inferring habits and dispositions;
- retaining observed actions;
- judging whether conduct is reasonable in light of what is known.

Prediction was therefore present in the initial conception of knowledge, rather than being introduced only in later revisions.

### 1.4 Learning

**Initial author position:** Learning goes beyond retaining information. It involves classifying existing information, connecting it meaningfully, and discovering relationships among what is known.

The initial discussion used learning a skill as an example. At this point, the boundary between knowledge and learning was not yet fully specified.

### 1.5 *شهرآگاهی*

**Initial author position:** The fifth stage was described through the analogy of a living, changing city. It involved treating what has been learned as usable and putting it into practice continuously.

The initial account therefore associated this stage with practical use, ongoing activity, and a dynamic environment. The later requirement to explain one's competence appropriately to others had not yet been stated in this first formulation.

---

## 2. First refinement: novelty is relative to the organism's prior awareness

The author clarified that the expression “it was not there before, and now it is” referred to the organism's awareness, not necessarily to the objective existence of the phenomenon.

Radio waves and radioactivity were used as examples: they existed before humans discovered them, but people were not previously aware of them in the relevant sense.

**Refined author position:** A phenomenon need not have newly come into existence for awareness to occur. What is new may be the organism's information about a phenomenon that already existed.

The discussion also distinguished everyday expressions such as “I found out,” “I recognize,” “I know,” and “I can do it.” These expressions helped motivate the five-stage distinction, but the author cautioned against confusing ordinary language with the technical meanings assigned to the framework's terms.

## 3. A proposed memory-based account of awareness

In response to the question of whether a measuring instrument that registers radiation is itself aware, the author proposed a biological mechanism:

1. A biological organism receives an event or information.
2. The received information is considered in relation to memory.
3. The organism detects that the information is new relative to what is available in memory.
4. The organism recognizes the novelty of the event.
5. The reception and registration of the event give rise to awareness of it.

This was presented as a proposed explanatory sequence, not as an experimentally established account of consciousness or memory.

### 3.1 Scope restriction

The author explicitly limited the immediate inquiry to biological organisms. The discussion of whether non-biological tools or artificial systems could be given analogous memory and awareness processes was deferred until stronger principles had been established in the biological domain.

Accordingly, this early discussion does **not** establish that a Geiger counter, camera, computer, or AI system is aware merely because it registers information.

---

## 4. Knowledge clarified through prediction

When asked whether someone who can predict another person's behavior is necessarily knowledgeable about that person, the author answered affirmatively and treated prediction as central to the definition of knowledge.

The author also used a staged example of learning to work with a computer:
- first becoming aware that such a device exists;
- then exploring its commands and features and becoming acquainted with its operating environment;
- later acquiring the ability to use it and program it;
- subsequently applying experience across different operating systems and conditions, diagnosing problems, building missing tools, and anticipating needs that have not yet been explicitly recognized.

The example clarified the intended progression:
- awareness of the existence of a subject;
- cognition or acquaintance with its features;
- knowledge expressed as usable competence and the ability to anticipate;
- learning expressed as deeper transfer, generalization, problem-solving, and the creation of new solutions.

Personal details from the original anecdote are intentionally omitted from this public research record.

---

## 5. Learning expanded beyond the discovery of relationships

The second discussion expanded the initial account of learning.

**Refined author position:** Learning can include:
- using prior experience in new circumstances;
- distinguishing how a subject behaves under different conditions;
- recognizing patterns across different systems or environments;
- anticipating needs before they are explicitly identified;
- creating tools or solutions to meet those needs.

This is a broader account than the initial emphasis on classifying information and discovering relationships. It does not erase the initial formulation; it records its subsequent expansion.

---

## 6. *شهرآگاهی* expanded as adaptive, goal-directed competence

The author clarified that *شهرآگاهی* involves more than putting learned information into practice. The stated elements included:

- a changing environment;
- knowledge applied in practice;
- adaptation;
- recognition of the situation;
- continuous performance;
- reaching a goal through more than one possible route.

The possibility of multiple routes to a goal is important: the fifth stage is not merely repetition of a single learned procedure. It involves adapting performance to the situation.

The author also associated advanced competence with the ability to use prior knowledge when entering a new subject, and with a constructive attitude toward the process of learning again.

At this point, however, the discussion had not yet fully formalized the later explicit rule that appropriate explanation to others is a necessary condition for *شهرآگاهی*.

---

## 7. Subject-specific stages and simultaneous differences across subjects

The author then clarified that *شهرآگاهی* is attained with respect to a particular subject, not as a universal status that applies to everything a person encounters.

A person may, at the same time:
- have *شهرآگاهی* in one subject;
- have cognition in another;
- have only awareness in a third.

Examples involving cities and natural languages were used to illustrate this point.

**Author position:** The five-stage status must be evaluated relative to a specified subject or dimension. A person's overall competence cannot be represented by assigning one stage to the person without identifying the subject.

This was a significant structural refinement of the original “steps” metaphor.

## 8. Transfer of prior capabilities across subjects

The author further proposed that entering a new subject does not always mean starting from an entirely unprepared state.

Examples involving related natural languages illustrated that prior knowledge in one language may help a person:
- recognize features in another language;
- infer some meaning without formal instruction;
- learn a related language more quickly.

The author explicitly favored a mixed or dimension-specific account over a single uniform starting point. In the discussion, this was expressed by selecting the option in which a person may have:
- awareness in some dimensions of a new subject;
- cognition in other dimensions;
- transferable knowledge in still others.

**Refined author position:** The stages are subject-specific, but capabilities acquired in one subject may influence the person's progression in another.

This does not mean that competence in one subject automatically establishes competence in a related subject. Transfer must be assessed by the particular dimension and capability involved.

---

## 9. Initial question of starting over in a new system

The author considered how an organism might experience entry into a completely new system, depending on its prior stage in another system.

The initial proposal distinguished three cases:
- an organism whose prior relation to the previous system was limited to awareness may not experience starting again as a significant loss;
- an organism with cognition, knowledge, or learning in the previous system may experience distress at having to begin again;
- an organism with *شهرآگاهی* may understand learning as valuable and may recognize that some prior knowledge can still be useful.

This was an early proposal about the relationship between prior competence, transfer, and the emotional experience of learning. It was not yet established as a universal psychological rule.

## 10. Historical development at a glance

| Concept | Initial formulation | First recorded refinement |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Noticing an event or difference | Novelty is relative to the organism's prior awareness; a memory-based mechanism was proposed |
| Cognition | Acquiring and recognizing features | Distinguished from knowledge through the computer-learning example |
| Knowledge | Using information to anticipate and judge | Prediction was explicitly defended as central |
| Learning | Classifying information and discovering relationships | Expanded to generalization, transfer, anticipation of unmet needs, and creating solutions |
| *شهرآگاهی* | Dynamic practical use of what has been learned | Expanded to adaptation, continuous performance, multiple routes to goals, and subject-specific attainment |
| Overall structure | Five ordered stages | Different subjects may place the same person at different stages simultaneously |

## 11. Questions left open at this point

The source discussion deliberately deferred several questions. The following remain historical questions to be tracked against later material:

1. What exactly counts as a sufficient comparison with memory for awareness?
2. Does every form of novelty become explicit awareness?
3. What are the precise boundaries between cognition, knowledge, and learning?
4. How can transferable capabilities be identified and assessed across subjects?
5. Can transfer cause a person to begin a new subject at different stages in different dimensions?
6. Is a positive attitude toward starting again part of the definition of *شهرآگاهی*, or a possible consequence of it?
7. How should the ordered five-stage progression be reconciled with parallel and recursive activity across dimensions?
